I'm Leia (they/them), a new media artist, creative technologist, and exhibit developer. I've been on the side of installing my own work as well as others', so here's a quick guide to making your work bit more robust.

---
A lot of artists make incredible work, but that work is generated at home or at the studio. You control the scenario, you know how to interact with it, and it doesn't have to deal with the real world. Putting it in the public is ... a little different. 

# installation basics 
	From 0 to 1

---
First, Let's break down the kind of installations you'll (likely) do. 

|  show  |  timeline  | what it's like   |
|----|----|----|
|  hackathon  |  <4 hours  |  you stand by the piece the whole time  |
|  showcase  |  1-3 days  |  you're still around to handle the piece, but maybe take breaks. it probably runs on your computer still  |
| exhibition | 3 days to 3 weeks | ==the turning point==|
| selling work | forever | *we're not gonna talk about this*|

"Exhibition" is where you stop babysitting your work, and hand it off to other people that will have to deal with it. This is the transition we'll talk about today, and how to think about it. 

---  

	==What's 0?==
	0 is ... 
	- a prototype! it works, but *on your computer*, and at max for like an hour 
	- it's held together by ... tape? hot glue? 
	- needs you to launch it

0 is where you've all been, and where most of your work sits. It doesn't make sense to go much farther when you're testing things out, experimenting, and exploring. 0 is all the artwork up through proof of concept. 

But then ... can you install your own, personal laptop at a gallery for 7 days? 

---

	==What's 1?==
	1 is ... 
	- working on a ==standalone computer== that will live in/at the installation site
	- robust to light bumps, ==structurally sound==, and is held together by nails, glue, or screws
	- ==launchable== in under 3 minutes by someone who is reading instructions off of a document
	- essentially an **installable object**

1 is what I would call *actually installable work*. You can put a 0 in a room and show it off, but won't fly at a gallery. 1 could live at a gallery, at least for a few days, without you babysitting it.

At the very least, you should have: 
- A computer or other hardware dedicated to this piece, at least for the time period of the exhibition. 
- A way to turn it on/off as needed. 

---
	If it's on a computer, it should be **auto-launching**. There's a bunch of ways of doing this. 
	- Mac: Automator
	- Windows: Task Scheduler 
	- Linux: Startup Applications 

	Set your computers to auto-log-in, skip passwords, and avoid updates. 
	Make sure the computer/hardware is *accessible* in case something goes wrong. 

Resource: there's a repo called [InstallationUp4Eva](https://github.com/laserpilot/Installation_Up_4evr) that's a bit old, but is a great place to start. It has forks for Windows and Linux as well. 

---
## Installable New Media Object Checklist 

	- [ ] On a dedicated computer/hardware
	- [ ] Can be turned off/on, muted, etc
	- [ ] Auto-launches when powered on
	- [ ] Structurally sound
	- [ ] Internals can be accessed for repair

## Installable New Media Object Checklist 

(ok, but these three are actually the big ones). 
	- [ ] ==On a dedicated computer/hardware==
	- [ ] ==Can be turned off/on, muted, etc==
	- [ ] ==Auto-launches when powered on==
	- [ ] Structurally sound
	- [ ] Internals can be accessed for repair

---
	 **Want to be the gallerist's favorite artist?**
	Get all of the above right, *and*
	- [ ] Brightness, volume, or other states can be adjusted 
	- [ ] It can be moved and reassembled quickly 
	- [ ] Bring extra equipment! Extension cords, backup batteries, gaff tape. 
	- [ ] It's easy to debug if something goes wrong

Each space will be different! If you can provide brightness controls, volume controls, or other tweaks, that lets your work shine in the best light in whatever environment it's in. 

It's inevitable that your work may need to be moved by someone who *isn't* you. Build enclosures that house all your hardware inside, and don't let wires or peripherals hang out in precarious places. 

And something will always go wrong. Be extra prepared and provide debug instructions; this can be as easy as "Reboot it" (autolaunch is KEY), or as much as "hey here's how you'd change the wifi on it if you need to".

# Proposing an Installation 

---
So when I usually teach this workshop, I'm teaching grad students who are just beginning their journey with art; I have to step them through the full experience of their work, have them think about how a person will actually interact with their work. 

	- What size is your object? 
	- What area do you want *around* your object? How should your object be oriented? 
	- What mode of interaction do you want from visitors? Are they sitting? Standing? Walking through? What space/infrastructure do they need to do that? 
	- What does your object need? Is it sensitive to light? Is it biological, sensitive to RF? 
	Think of the senses; sight, smell, sound, taste, touch. How does your work engage those? 
	- How should people first see your work? Should they be eye level? Need to peek? Around the corner? 
	- What about lighting? Sound isolation? 
	- Is it photographable? Should it be? 
	- Can people touch it?

---
So here's a basic way to think about how you should build your proposal:

	include in your proposal ...
	- what is it *actually*
	- what does it need space-wise
	- equipment you need provided
	- maintenance requirements

What is it *actually*?
- Describe your piece FULLY. Is it a computer? A raspberry pi in a box? A painting with a light shining on it? A single flower rotating on a motor? 

What does it need space-wise?
- Physical installation: Is it mounted from the ceiling? From a wall? Does it sit on the floor, or a pedestal, or a shelf? 
- Environmental needs: Does it need to be in a lit area, or dark? Sound isolation? 

Equipment you need provided
- Pedestals, lights, speakers, projection surfaces
- Access to power, wifi networks, or other digital infrastructure

Maintenance requirements
- Will someone need to refresh it? Charge something? 
- Does it need to be stocked with materials, like paper or ink? 
- How do you start/stop the piece? 
- What should they do if something goes wrong?

## So how do you communicate all this?

That's a lot to think about from your side. Your job is to get all of this information over to a curator/gallerist, who is looking at tens or hundreds of other proposals just like yours. 

This is the same process I go through at work, from both sides.
